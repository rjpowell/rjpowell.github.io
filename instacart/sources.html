FAQ
Why Phoenix suburbs?
Because it's the path of least resistance:
  • Weather: 299 sunny days/year. Drones don't fly in rain, snow, or high wind. Phoenix has almost none of that.
  • Regulatory: Arizona already has FAA drone delivery approvals. Wing and DroneUp operate there.
  • Use case fit: Suburban = longer delivery distances, lower shopper density. Exactly where drone economics might work.
Could also consider Dallas-Fort Worth or parts of Florida. But Phoenix has the cleanest conditions for a first test.
Who are we getting RFPs from?
Three providers worth talking to:
  • Wing (Alphabet): Most mature. FAA Part 135 certified. Already delivers for Walmart. Aerial drones.
  • Nuro: Sidewalk/road robots, not aerial. Different tech, potentially more payload. Kroger partnership.
  • DroneUp: Walmart's primary partner. Deep retail experience. Aerial drones.
The RFP is to get real pricing, not marketing numbers. If nobody can hit <$25/delivery, we stop.
Why a Discovery Sprint instead of just doing a pilot?
Because pilots are expensive and hard to kill. A 6-month pilot with Wing or Nuro could cost $500K+ in integration, ops, and marketing. Discovery costs <$50K and answers the same questions:
  • Do customers actually want this? (Fake door)
  • Will shoppers revolt? (Survey)
  • Can providers hit reasonable pricing? (RFP)
  • Will retailers opt in? (Portal CTA)
If any of those fail, you've saved $450K+ and 6 months. If they all pass, you go into the pilot with confidence instead of hope.
Why not build in-house?
Instacart is a marketplace, not an aerospace company. Building drones requires:
  • FAA certification (takes years)
  • Hardware manufacturing
  • Airspace management software
  • Maintenance and repair infrastructure
Amazon tried this with billions of dollars and 10+ years. They're still at $63/delivery. Partnering lets Instacart test the market without betting the company on hardware.
What about shopper backlash?
This is the biggest brand risk. But the framing matters:
  • Threat framing: "Drones are taking your deliveries" → Bad.
  • Benefit framing: "Drones take small, low-tip orders so you can focus on larger, higher-tip orders" → Maybe fine?
The shopper survey tests both framings. If shoppers hate both, we stop. If they accept the benefit framing, there's a path forward. We don't guess—we measure.
Isn't 1M Walmart deliveries a big deal?
No. Context matters:
  • Walmart does 3.5 billion same/next-day deliveries annually.
  • 1M drone deliveries = 0.03% of that volume.
  • Only 70 of 4,600 stores (1.5%) have drone capability.
  • Per-delivery cost is still high (estimated $15-25+).
The headline sounds impressive. The math says it's a rounding error and likely still losing money.
What orders are actually drone-eligible?
Strict constraints:
  • Weight: ≤5 lbs (rules out most full grocery runs)
  • Items: ≤10 items
  • Distance: ≤3 miles from store
  • Weather: Clear, low wind
  • Location: Customer must have landing space (no apartments)
This might be 5-15% of Instacart orders. The technical review in discovery validates the exact percentage.
What's the kill threshold for fake door click rate?
<2% = stop. That means fewer than 2 in 100 eligible customers chose drone delivery when offered. If people don't even click a free, faster option, there's no demand to serve. Save your money.
